How to track your physio exercises at home (and what to write down)

By Retune PhysioUpdated 5 November 2026

In short

  • Research on habits and goals finds that people who regularly check their progress are more likely to reach their goal, especially when they write it down.
  • Keep it short: the date, what you did (full or a short version), how it felt, and anything that got in the way. Under a minute is enough.
  • A record is for noticing patterns and talking to your physio, not for judging yourself. A missed day is information, not a failure.

Your physio gives you a sheet of exercises and says "do these most days". Then life happens. A week later it's hard to remember whether you did them three times or five, which ones you skipped, or why. A simple record fixes that, and it doesn't need to be complicated.

Why keeping a record helps

Checking how you're going against a goal is one of the best-studied tools in behaviour change. A large review of 138 experiments found that when people were helped to monitor their progress more often, they were more likely to reach their goal. The effect was bigger when people physically recorded their progress, rather than just thinking about it, and when they shared it with someone else.1

An earlier review of programs to increase physical activity and healthy eating found something similar: of all the techniques used, self-monitoring explained the most difference between programs that worked and programs that didn't. It worked best alongside other steps such as setting a specific goal and reviewing it.2

For physio exercises specifically, the research on what helps people keep going is promising but mixed, and recording what you do is usually one part of a bigger approach rather than the whole answer.3 Reviews also point to confidence and support as important for keeping up home exercises.4 A record helps with both: you can see what you've managed, and your physio can see it too.

What to write down

The best record is one you'll actually keep. Aim for something you can fill in within a minute of finishing.

  • The date. Or just tick the day on a weekly grid.
  • What you did. The full session, or a short version (for example one set of each instead of three). Both count.
  • Anything different from the plan. Fewer reps, an exercise you skipped, a lighter weight. A word or two is enough.
  • How it felt. Easy, about right, or hard. This is often more useful to your physio than exact numbers.
  • A pain score, if your physio has asked for one. Some physios use a 0 to 10 scale to see how things change over a few weeks. If yours hasn't mentioned it, a note like "knee sore after stairs" is fine.
  • What got in the way. On days you didn't do them: busy, forgot, sore, unsure how. Over a few weeks, this shows you what to fix.

What you don't need to track

  • Everything. If filling in the record takes longer than a few minutes, it won't last. Cut it back.
  • Perfection. A rough record you keep is more useful than a detailed one you abandon after a week.
  • Numbers you can't act on. Calories, step counts and heart rate can be interesting, but they're not what your physio needs to adjust your home program.

Paper, phone or app?

Any of them work. A paper log on the fridge is free and easy to bring to an appointment. A phone calendar or notes app is always with you. An exercise tracking app can add reminders and a summary. Pick the one you're most likely to keep using. There's a fuller comparison in exercise diary or app: which suits you?, and a free printable physio exercise log if you'd like to start on paper.

How to use your record

  • Glance at it once a week. Which days worked? Which didn't, and why? Small changes, like moving your exercises to straight after breakfast, often come from this.
  • Bring it to your next review. It gives your physio a much clearer picture than trying to remember, and makes the appointment more useful. See what to bring to a physio review.
  • Use it to plan, not to judge. A gap in the record isn't a failure. It's a sign something got in the way, and that's worth knowing. If you've missed a few days, see how to get going again.

When to check in sooner

If your record shows pain building over several sessions, a big change in how an exercise feels, or new symptoms, don't wait for your next appointment. Stop the exercise that's causing it and check with your physio or doctor.
