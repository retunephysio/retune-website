Why it's hard to stick with exercise (it isn't willpower)

Written and reviewed by a registered physiotherapistReviewed 29 October 2026Updated 29 October 2026

In short

  • Motivation goes up and down. Plans that rely on feeling motivated tend to fade when life gets busy.
  • Habits work differently: repeating something in the same situation makes it more automatic, so it needs less effort to start.
  • People who see exercise as part of who they are tend to do more of it, and that identity can grow from small, repeated sessions.

Most people who stop exercising didn't decide to stop. They meant to do it tomorrow, then the day after, and then a few weeks had gone. If that sounds familiar, it doesn't mean you lack willpower. It's how most people's brains handle a new behaviour that isn't a habit yet.

Motivation is a poor engine for a routine

Motivation is real, but it changes with your mood, energy, sleep, work and pain. A plan that only works on motivated days will stall on the others. Research on home exercise programmes finds that intention and self-motivation do matter,4 but they aren't the whole story. Lots of people fully intend to exercise and still don't get to it.

Habits do the heavy lifting

A habit, in the way researchers use the word, is something you do automatically because of the situation you're in, not because you've talked yourself into it. Brushing your teeth after breakfast is a good example: you don't weigh it up each morning.

Habits form when you repeat an action in a consistent situation. Over time the situation itself starts to prompt the action, and it takes less thought to get started.1 Researchers suggest this is why habits can keep a behaviour going even when motivation dips.1

In a study following 111 new gym members for 12 weeks, the people who formed an exercise habit had kept their routine consistent and simple, and their surroundings supported it. Enjoying the exercise also helped.2

"I'm just not an exercise person"

Many people say this, and it's worth taking seriously. Researchers have looked at "exercise identity": whether people see physical activity as part of who they are. Across many studies, a stronger exercise identity was linked with doing more physical activity.3

The encouraging part is that identity isn't fixed. It tends to follow what you actually do. Each session is a small piece of proof. You don't have to believe you're "an active person" before you start. Over weeks, the evidence can do the convincing.

Other things that get in the way

Reviews of people doing physiotherapy exercises have found other common barriers:4,5

  • low confidence that you can do the exercises, or fit them in;
  • not having been active before;
  • little support from people around you;
  • seeing lots of barriers, such as time, cost or tiredness;
  • pain during exercise, low mood or anxiety.

None of these are personal failings, and several can be worked on. Talking to your physio about pain during exercise, or about low confidence, is a good place to start.

Putting it together

  • Lower the bar to start. A small session you'll actually do beats a big one you keep putting off.
  • Same time, same place, same trigger. Tie it to something you already do every day. See the "After I…" plan.
  • Expect off days. Have a short version ready, and get back to it the next day.
  • Notice the evidence. Keep a simple record of what you've done, so the proof is easy to see.
