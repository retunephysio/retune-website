A review appointment is a chance to check how things are going, update your exercises and sort out anything that's been getting in the way. They're often short, and it's easy to walk out and remember the question you meant to ask. A few minutes of preparation helps.

What to bring

  • Your current exercises. The sheet, photos, videos or app you've been using, so your physio can see exactly what you've been doing.
  • A simple record of what you've done. Roughly how many sessions a week, and which exercises. It doesn't need to be perfect: a calendar, notebook or app is fine. It gives a clearer picture than trying to remember.
  • Notes on how it's felt. Which exercises feel easy, hard or uncomfortable, and how your symptoms have changed. If you've noticed patterns, such as worse in the morning or after sitting, write them down.
  • What's got in the way. Time, pain, motivation, not being sure you're doing it right. These are normal, and they're useful for your physio to know.
  • Your goal, in your own words. "Walk the dog without stopping", "pick up my grandkids", "get back to tennis". It helps your physio aim the plan at what matters to you.
  • Any letters or results from your GP or specialist since your last visit.

If you have more than one problem

It's common to have a few things going on: the knee you came in for, plus a sore shoulder and some back stiffness. It's worth mentioning all of them briefly at the start, because sometimes they're connected and your physio will want to know.

But a short appointment can't do a proper job of everything at once. If the time gets split into a few minutes on each of three areas, there's often not enough left to look at any one of them carefully or make a clear plan. You can leave with three half-answers instead of one good one.

What usually works better:

  • Pick your main concern before you arrive. Ask yourself: if only one thing improved, which would make the biggest difference to your week?
  • Mention the others in a sentence each, then let your physio decide whether they're linked or can wait.
  • Ask what to do about the rest. Some things can be looked at next time, some may be simple to add to your home plan, and some may need a different appointment.

Questions worth asking

  • Am I doing these exercises correctly? Can you watch me do them?
  • Which exercises matter most if I'm short on time?
  • What should a short version look like on a busy or sore day?
  • How should I progress them, and when?
  • What's normal to feel during and after, and what means I should stop or check in?
  • When should I come back, or what would mean I should book in sooner?

Why it's worth it

Reviews of people doing physio exercises at home have found that confidence in doing the exercises, and in fitting them in, is one of the things linked with keeping them up.2 A review where your physio watches your technique and answers your questions is a good way to build that confidence.

Researchers have tested several ways to help people stick with physio exercises, including written instructions, goal setting, follow-up and activity monitoring with feedback. Results are promising but mixed.1,3 For people with hip or knee osteoarthritis, a review found that follow-up "booster" sessions helped people keep up their exercises.4

Leave with a plan you can follow

Before you go, check you know:

  1. which exercises to do, and how many sets and repetitions;
  2. how often, and when in your day you'll do them;
  3. your short version for busy or sore days;
  4. what to watch for, and when to get back in touch;
  5. when your next review is, if you're having one.

If anything isn't clear, ask for it written down. It's much easier to follow a plan you understand.
