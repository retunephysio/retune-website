You've probably heard that it takes 21 days to form a habit. It's a tidy number, but it isn't what research has found. The real answer is longer, and it varies a lot from person to person. That's good news in a way: if a new routine still feels like hard work after three weeks, nothing has gone wrong.

What the research found

In a study from University College London, 96 people each chose a daily eating, drinking or activity habit to build and tracked it for 12 weeks. They rated how automatic it felt each day.1

  • The middle figure for reaching close to peak automaticity was 66 days.
  • Individuals ranged from 18 to 254 days.
  • Habits felt more automatic quickly at first, then the gains slowed down.

A 2024 review from the University of South Australia pulled together 20 studies of health habits, including physical activity.2 Studies that reported a typical (median) time found about 59 to 66 days. Average times were longer, at around 106 to 154 days, because some people took much longer. Individual times ranged from 4 to 335 days.

What about exercise specifically?

Exercise is more complex than drinking a glass of water, so it may take longer. A Canadian study followed 111 new gym members for 12 weeks.3 It found that exercising at least four times a week for six weeks was the minimum linked with an exercise habit forming. Keeping the routine consistent and simple, having supportive surroundings and enjoying it all helped.

These were people going to the gym, not doing rehab exercises, so the numbers won't fit everyone exactly. The pattern is still useful: frequent, consistent repetition matters more than how hard each session is.

What this means for you

  • Think in months, not weeks. Plan to keep it going for at least two months before you expect it to feel automatic.
  • Frequency matters. Doing something often, in the same situation, builds a habit faster than doing it occasionally.4 If your physio gave you a schedule, stick to it. If you're building your own routine, more often and smaller tends to work better than rarely and big.
  • Expect early gains, then a plateau. The first few weeks often feel like the hardest work, and then it gets easier more slowly.
  • An off day isn't a reset. In the UCL study, missing one opportunity didn't make much difference to how the habit developed.1 See getting back on track after missed sessions.

How you'll know it's becoming a habit

Researchers often measure habit with four simple statements.5 You might notice that your routine is something you:

  • do automatically;
  • do without having to consciously remember;
  • do without thinking;
  • start before you realise you're doing it.

If those start to ring true, the routine is taking care of itself more and needs less effort from you.
