While you're seeing a physio, there's a plan, someone checking in, and an appointment coming up that gives you a reason to keep going. When the appointments finish, all of that goes quiet. The exercise sheet goes on the fridge, life gets busy, and the routine is up to you.

If your exercises have slipped since then, you're in good company. This page covers what research has found about why that happens, and what seems to help.

How common is it?

Very. A systematic review of home-based physical therapy found that not keeping up with a home programme can reach 70%.1 Another review put estimates at up to 50%.2 The numbers vary a lot between studies because they measure it in different ways. The pattern is the same, though: doing exercises on your own, without appointments, is hard for most people.

Why it happens (and why it isn't a willpower problem)

Researchers have looked at what separates people who keep going from people who don't. The factors that come up most often are:1,3

  • Confidence that you can do the exercises and fit them in (researchers call this self-efficacy).
  • Past exercise habits. People who were already active before tend to keep going.
  • Support from family, friends or others.
  • How many barriers you see, such as time, tiredness or not knowing where to start.
  • Pain during exercise, and low mood or anxiety.

Most of these aren't about trying harder. If exercise has never been part of your week, each session is a fresh decision, and decisions are easy to put off. That's why the ideas below focus on making the routine easier to start, not on finding more motivation.

What tends to help

1. Make it small and tie it to your day

Habits form when you repeat something in the same situation until it starts to feel automatic.5 In a study of new gym members, keeping things simple and consistent were among the factors linked with a habit forming.6

A practical way to do this is to link your exercises to something you already do every day: "After I pour my morning coffee, I'll do my exercises." There's more on this in the "After I…" plan.

2. Have a plan for busy or sore days

Some days won't go to plan. In a well-known study of habit formation, missing one opportunity didn't make much difference to how the habit developed over time.7 What matters more is getting back to it.

It can help to agree on a short version with your physio before you finish, such as one set of each exercise. That way a busy or sore day still keeps the routine alive, without you guessing what's safe to cut.

3. Keep track of what you've done

Recording your sessions lets you see your own progress, which many people find motivating. Reviews of ways to help people stick with physio exercises have looked at activity monitors and feedback, written exercise instructions, goal setting and follow-up sessions.2,8 The research is promising but mixed, so it's worth trying what suits you: a calendar, a note on the fridge or an app.

4. Connect it to something you care about

"Do my exercises" is easy to skip. "Get back to long walks with the dog" is easier to hold on to. Research on exercise identity has found that people who see physical activity as part of who they are tend to be more active.9 You don't need to feel like "an exercise person" yet. Each session you do is a small piece of evidence that you can be.

5. Ask about a check-in

For people with hip or knee osteoarthritis, a review found that follow-up "booster" sessions with a physiotherapist helped people keep up their exercises.4 If you're worried about keeping going on your own, it's reasonable to ask your physio whether a review appointment a few weeks after you finish would suit you.

When to check back with your physio

Keeping up your exercises on your own doesn't mean you're on your own. It's worth getting in touch if:

  • pain is getting worse, or you have new symptoms;
  • you're not sure how to do an exercise, or whether it's still right for you;
  • the exercises feel much too easy or too hard, and you're not sure how to change them.
