What the research says

The "After I…" plan: tie your exercises to something you already do

Written and reviewed by a registered physiotherapistReviewed 29 October 2026Updated 29 October 2026

In short

  • An "After I…, I'll…" plan links a new behaviour to something you already do every day.
  • Researchers call these implementation intentions. Studies find they help people follow through on what they intend, including physical activity.
  • Pick a cue that happens every day at a similar time and place, keep the first step small, and adjust if it isn't working.

"I'll do my exercises more" is a good intention, but it doesn't say when, where or how. Most people's days are full of moments where it could happen and none where it has to. An "After I…" plan fixes that by giving the exercise a set moment in your day.

After I pour my morning coffee, I'll do my knee exercises.

Why it works

Researchers call this kind of plan an implementation intention: an "if this happens, then I'll do that" plan. A large review of studies across many kinds of goals found that people who made these plans were more likely to follow through than people who only set a goal.1 A review focused on physical activity found a smaller but real benefit, mainly in studies where the plan was reinforced over time.2

There are two likely reasons. First, the cue does the remembering for you: when you pour the coffee, the plan comes to mind. Second, repeating the same action after the same cue is how habits form, so over weeks it starts to feel automatic.3 You may also have heard this called "habit stacking".

How to set one up

  1. Pick a cue you never miss. Something that happens every day, at about the same time and in the same place: making coffee, brushing your teeth, getting home from work, putting the kettle on.
  2. Pick where you'll do it. If the cue is in the kitchen, can you do the exercises there? Fewer steps between the cue and the exercise helps.
  3. Make the first step tiny. "Put my mat down" or "do the first exercise" is enough to start. Simple routines are linked with habits forming.5
  4. Say it or write it down in your own words: "After I…, I'll…".
  5. Plan for the hard days. Add a second line, such as "If I'm short on time, I'll do one set of each."

Which cue works best?

There's no single best cue, but a few patterns show up in research:

  • Self-chosen beats assigned. A 2024 review found that habits people chose themselves, and morning routines, tended to become stronger.4
  • Consistent beats clever. A cue that happens every day is more useful than one that only happens some days.3
  • Tie it to the action, not the clock. "At 7am" works if your mornings are regular. "After I brush my teeth" still works on the days you sleep in.

Examples

  • After I pour my morning coffee, I'll do my calf raises while it cools.
  • After I brush my teeth at night, I'll do my shoulder exercises.
  • After I get home from work, I'll go for a 20-minute walk before I sit down.
  • After I put the kettle on, I'll do ten sit-to-stands.

If your exercises came from a physio, keep doing them the way they showed you. The plan changes when you do them, not what you do.

If it isn't working

That's useful information, not failure. Usually the cue is the problem: it doesn't happen every day, or you're in the wrong place when it does. Try a different cue for a week or two. If the exercises themselves are the barrier, for example because of pain or not having time, that's worth raising with your physio.
