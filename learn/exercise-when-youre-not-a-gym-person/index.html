Starting from zero: exercise when you're "not a gym person"

By Retune PhysioUpdated 29 October 2026

In short

  • Guidelines say any activity is better than none, and it doesn't have to happen in a gym. Walking, gardening and stairs count.
  • People keep going longer with activity they choose and enjoy. How exercise feels during it is linked with whether people do it again.
  • Seeing yourself as someone who exercises is linked with doing more of it, and that identity can grow from small, repeated sessions.

Lots of people say "I'm just not a gym person", often after a few attempts that didn't stick. The good news is that you don't have to become one. Being active and going to the gym aren't the same thing, and the research on starting from zero is more encouraging than most people expect.

You don't need a gym

Australia's guidelines say any physical activity is better than none, and suggest that people who aren't active now start with some activity and build up gradually.1 Walking, cycling, gardening, dancing, swimming, taking the stairs and carrying shopping all count. So do exercises at home with no equipment.

Starting from zero also means even a small change is a meaningful one. In a large review of studies using activity monitors, the biggest difference in health risk was between the least active people and those doing a bit more, even at light intensity.6 These are observational findings, so they show a link rather than proof, but they're a reason not to wait until you can do "proper" exercise.

Pick something you want to do

A review of 66 studies on exercise motivation found that people who exercise because they value it or enjoy it, rather than because they feel they should, tend to keep going for longer. Enjoyment was more closely linked with sticking with it over the long term.2

How exercise feels while you're doing it seems to matter too. A review found that people who felt better during moderate exercise were more likely to be active later on.3 If every session feels like punishment, it's harder to come back.

Some questions that can help you choose:

  • What did you enjoy moving for as a kid? A sport, a bike, dancing, swimming?
  • Do you prefer being alone, with one other person or in a group?
  • Indoors or outdoors? Music, podcast or quiet?
  • Is there something you'd like to be able to do, like a bushwalk or keeping up with your kids?

Start smaller than you think

When people start from zero, the temptation is to make up for lost time. A plan that's too big often gets dropped after a week or two. In a study of new gym members, the people who formed an exercise habit kept their routine simple and consistent, and enjoyed what they were doing.5

  • Pick a size you could do on a bad day. A 10-minute walk is a fine place to start.
  • Give it a set time. Tie it to something you already do. See the "After I…" plan.
  • Build up slowly. Add a few minutes or another day once it feels routine, not before.
  • Keep it comfortable at first. You should be able to talk while you're doing it. Pushing hard can wait.

The identity part

"Not a gym person" is an identity statement, and identity matters. A review of many studies found that people who see physical activity as part of who they are tend to be more active.4

But identity doesn't have to come first. It tends to follow what you actually do. You don't have to believe you're "an active person" before you start. Each walk or session is a small piece of evidence, and over weeks the evidence starts to change the story.

Before you start

If you have a health condition, have been told to be careful with exercise, are pregnant, or get chest pain, dizziness or unusual breathlessness when you're active, talk to your GP or physio first. They can help you find a safe starting point.
