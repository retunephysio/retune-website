What the research says

Exercise diary or app: which suits you for your physio exercises?

Written and reviewed by a registered physiotherapistReviewed 9 November 2026Updated 5 November 2026

In short

  • Two Australian trials found people reported slightly better adherence when their home exercises came through an app or web system rather than usual handouts. How much that matters for recovery isn't clear yet.
  • Paper, phone reminders and apps can all work. The best one is the one you'll keep using.
  • If you choose an app, check where your data is stored, whether it has ads or tracking, and whether you can share a summary and delete your account.

Once you're doing physio exercises at home, it helps to keep some kind of record. Writing progress down is linked with a better chance of reaching a goal.4 But should that be a notebook, your phone's calendar, or an exercise tracking app? Here's what the research says and how to choose.

What the trials found

Two Australian randomised trials have compared delivering home exercise programs digitally with the usual approach:

  • In a Sydney hospital trial of 80 people with musculoskeletal conditions, those who got their program on an app reported slightly better adherence after four weeks than those given a paper handout. The app group also received phone calls and motivational text messages, so it's hard to know how much came from the app itself. The authors noted that the clinical importance of the difference was unclear.1
  • In a trial of 305 people seeing physiotherapists in private practice, those whose program was set up with a web-based exercise system reported better adherence and more confidence to do their exercises after three weeks, compared with their physio's usual method. Again, the authors said the clinical relevance still needs to be established.2

A review of "connected health" tools for home exercise, such as apps, sensors and websites, found they show promise for helping people keep up their exercises, but that the evidence was still limited.3

In short: digital tools may help a little, mostly through reminders, clear instructions and feeling supported. They're not magic, and a paper record you keep is better than an app you don't open.

The options compared

Good forWatch out for
Paper log or diaryFree, private, no login, easy to bring to appointments, nothing to learnNo reminders, easy to lose, harder to see changes over several weeks
Phone calendar or remindersFree, always with you, reliable remindersDoesn't record what you did or how it felt; reminders are easy to swipe away
Your clinic's exercise appYour physio sets it up, often with videos of each exerciseUsually needs a clinic code, and access may end when you finish treatment
A standalone exercise tracker appReminders, a history you can look back on, often a summary to sharePrivacy varies a lot; some apps rely on points or counts that reset, which some people find discouraging

How to choose

  • If you like paper, use paper. Our free printable physio exercise log fits a week on one page.
  • If you forget, add reminders. Tie them to something you already do, like "after breakfast". See the "After I…" plan.
  • If your physio uses an app, try theirs first. It's set up for you, and they can see how it's going.
  • If you've finished treatment, or want something that's yours to keep, a standalone app or a paper log both work well.

What to check before choosing an app

  • Where your data is stored, and whether the app has advertising or tracking tools. Your exercises and pain notes are health information. The privacy policy should say this plainly.
  • Whether you can enter your own plan exactly as your physio wrote it, without the app changing the amounts.
  • A short version for hard days. Doing something small should count, rather than being marked as a miss.
  • How it handles missed days. If a missed day wipes out a count you've built up, some people give up altogether. Look for something that shows what you've done, not what you've missed.
  • A summary you can share with your physio, on your terms.
  • An easy way to delete your account and data.

Whatever you choose, the aim is the same: a quick, honest record that helps you keep going and gives your physio a clear picture. For what's worth writing down, see how to track your physio exercises at home.
