It's late, you're tired, and you only have ten minutes. Is it worth doing anything, or should you wait until you can do it properly? For a long time, the message was that activity only counted if you did at least 10 minutes at a time. The research has moved on.

What the guidelines say now

Australia's physical activity guidelines open with a simple line: doing any physical activity is better than doing none. If you're not active now, they suggest starting with some activity and building up gradually.1 The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines say the same thing: some activity is better than none.2

The full recommendation for adults is 2.5 to 5 hours of moderate activity (or 1.25 to 2.5 hours of vigorous activity) a week, plus muscle-strengthening on at least 2 days.1 That's a target to work towards, not a bar you have to clear before anything counts.

Do short bouts count?

A review done for the 2018 US physical activity guidelines looked at exactly this question. It found that activity in bouts of any length, including under 10 minutes, was linked with better health, and suggested that total activity matters more than how it's broken up.3

Two larger studies point the same way:

  • A review that pooled data from eight studies of over 36,000 middle-aged and older adults, who wore activity monitors, found that more activity of any intensity, even light, was linked with a lower risk of dying early. The biggest difference was between the least active people and those doing a bit more.4
  • A UK study of about 25,000 adults who didn't exercise found that a few short bursts of vigorous everyday activity (1 to 2 minutes each, such as walking briskly uphill or carrying shopping up stairs) were linked with a lower risk of dying early.5

These are observational studies. They show a link, not proof that short bouts cause the benefit, and the people in them may differ in other ways. But together with the guidelines, they make it hard to argue that a short session "doesn't count".

Why short sessions matter for habits

There's a second reason short sessions are worth doing, beyond the activity itself. Habits form by repeating something in the same situation until it starts to feel automatic.6 On a hard day, the choice is often between a short session and nothing. The short session keeps the routine going: same cue, same place, still showing up. Skipping makes the next session a fresh decision again.

In a well-known habit study, missing a single opportunity didn't make much difference to how the habit developed.6 So a skipped day isn't a disaster either. But a short version gives you a middle option between "full session" and "nothing".

What a short version can look like

  • one set of each exercise instead of three;
  • just the one or two exercises that matter most;
  • a 10-minute walk instead of 30;
  • a few minutes of brisk stairs or hills during the day.

In my experience as a physio, the people who stay consistent are often the ones who make the short version part of the plan, not a fallback they feel bad about. On some busy days the short version is simply a shorter, easier session. On others, when you're really short on time, it can mean doing fewer sets but working a bit harder in each one. Which of these suits you, and how hard is hard enough, is something to agree with your physio first.

If your exercises came from a physio, ask them what the short version should be. They know why each exercise is there and which ones matter most for you. Agreeing on it in advance means you're not guessing on the day.

When short isn't the right answer

A short version is for busy or tired days. If you're doing less because of pain, or pain is getting worse, check with your physio or GP rather than just cutting back on your own.
